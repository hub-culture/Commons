# Chocó Pacific guardians: a community project for the Global Commons

*In design, 17 November 2026. Started at the Hub Culture Climate Campus leadership dinner, COP31 Antalya, on Monday 16 November, when people working with communities on Colombia's Chocó Pacific coast described what those communities need. Nothing here is built yet, and no community has signed on. This note sets out a design for the communities of the coast to shape, change or turn down.*

## Why

The Chocó coast, on Colombia's Pacific, is one of the most biodiverse places on Earth, on land and at sea. Speakers at the dinner described it as the second most biodiverse marine corridor on the planet. Its communities are Afro-descendant and Indigenous, and many hold their lands collectively.

The coast already has protected areas built with its communities, including:

- **El Encanto de los Manglares del Bajo Baudó**, an integrated management district declared by Codechocó in 2017: about 314,000 hectares, two-thirds of it marine, with the largest mangrove area in Chocó. Its marine part protects the near-shore from dragnets and other destructive fishing. Its rules for use are discussed with the people who live there. ([WCS Colombia](https://colombia.wcs.org/en-us/Wild-Places/Pacífico/DRMI-ENCANTO-DE-LOS-MANGLARES-DEL-BAJO-BAUDÓ/En-proceso-de-paz-con-su-territorio-en-US.aspx), [Nature and Culture International](https://natureandculture.org/colombias-largest-regional-protected-area/))
- **Colinas y Lomas Submarinas del Pacífico Norte**, a deep-ocean district created in 2022, part of the Eastern Tropical Pacific Marine Corridor (CMAR). ([International Parks](https://www.internationalparks.org/colombia/Colinas%20y%20Lomas%20Submarinas%20del%20Pac%C3%ADfico%20Norte))
- **Utría National Natural Park** and the **Tribugá Gulf**, with whale sharks, hammerheads and 22 species of shark recorded. ([KBA](https://www.keybiodiversityareas.org/site/factsheet/14454), [Shark and Ray Areas](https://sharkrayareas.org/?p=3119))

Protected areas give the coast rules. What the speakers said is missing is the base those rules stand on.

## What the communities need, in the order they need it

As it was put at the dinner: no conservation or economic project works until the community's vital needs are met.

1. **Vital needs: water, connectivity and energy.** These come first.
2. **Education**, once those are in place.
3. **Tourism**, which connects people to the territory and to its real purpose.

Underneath all three, the goal is the community's own capacity: **self-sustaining economic models run by the community**, and conservation led by the people who already do it, as **guardians on the front line of the Pacific coast**.

## What the project would be

A community on Cloud Mirror, governed by the people of the coast, that funds their vital needs first and pays them for the guarding they already do.

1. **Governance first.** The community councils and Indigenous authorities hold the governing seats from the start. They write the charter in Propel, set its thresholds, and can lock parts of it. Their consent, and control of their own data, are built in, following the [Indigenous and community lands layer](indigenous-lands-layer.md).
2. **Vital needs as the first projects.**
   - **Energy and connectivity:** a community edge mesh, run as a component of a hub, with solar power and satellite internet. The same link carries the community's monitoring data out and its payments in.
   - **Water:** a funded first project chosen by the community, with what was promised and what arrived tracked openly.
3. **A guardian economy.**
   - **Blue carbon:** mangroves enter the [open blue carbon registry](blue-carbon-registry.md) with the community recorded as their keepers and first claimant on any value.
   - **Ocean data:** guardians run sensors and monitoring that feed the Ocean Data Initiative, and are paid for the data.
   - **First claim on value:** inside the territory, the people of the coast hold first claim on Ven Oxygen, DIRT or any other value layer, and can decide nothing is launched.
4. **On the map.** With consent, the territory appears on Gaia next to its marine protected areas, blue carbon sites and the payments meant to reach it.
5. **Then education and tourism**, when the community chooses: learning tied to the territory, and visits, including Hub Culture expeditions, that bring people to the place on the community's terms.

## Rules

- **Nothing without consent.** No data, boundary or name is published, and no value layer is launched, without the free, prior and informed consent of the communities concerned.
- **Needs before markets.** Water, energy and connectivity are funded before any credit, token or tourism product.
- **The evidence rule.** Claims of protection, and payments made, settle only when independent lines of evidence agree, and the community's own monitoring is one of them.
- **Exportable.** The charter is written so other coastal communities can adopt it.

## Questions for the communities

1. Who speaks for the communities: which community councils and which Indigenous authorities?
2. Which community or council would pilot it?
3. Of water, energy and connectivity, which is most urgent, and where?
4. What monitoring do communities already do, and what would they want paid for?
5. What should never appear on a public map?
